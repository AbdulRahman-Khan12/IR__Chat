# The Turing Test

In 1950 the British mathematician Alan Turing published a paper titled "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in the journal Mind. Rather than asking whether a machine can think, a question he considered too vague to answer, Turing proposed a practical substitute that he called the imitation game.

In the test a human judge holds a typed conversation with two hidden participants, one human and one machine, and has to decide which is which. If the judge cannot reliably tell them apart, the machine is said to have passed. The exchange is restricted to text so that the machine is not penalised for lacking a human voice or body.

Turing predicted that within about fifty years a computer with roughly a hundred megabytes of storage would fool an average judge for five minutes about thirty per cent of the time.

Critics argue that the test rewards imitation rather than understanding. The philosopher John Searle made this case in 1980 with the Chinese Room argument, which claims that manipulating symbols according to rules is not the same as grasping their meaning. The Loebner Prize, funded by Hugh Loebner and first held in 1991, ran an annual restricted version of the test for almost three decades.
