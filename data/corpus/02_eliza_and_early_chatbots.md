# ELIZA and the First Chatbots

ELIZA was written between 1964 and 1966 by Joseph Weizenbaum at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and is usually described as the first chatbot. ELIZA had no model of the world. It matched the user's sentence against a set of patterns and rewrote it into a question.

Its best known script, DOCTOR, imitated a Rogerian psychotherapist, a style of therapy in which the therapist mostly reflects the patient's own words back at them. A typical rule matched the pattern "I am X" and answered "How long have you been X?", swapping pronouns so that "my" became "your".

Weizenbaum was disturbed by the result. Users, including his own secretary, formed emotional attachments to the program and insisted it understood them. He set out his objections in the 1976 book Computer Power and Human Reason.

In 1972 the psychiatrist Kenneth Colby at Stanford University built PARRY, which simulated a patient with paranoid schizophrenia. PARRY was more sophisticated than ELIZA because it carried internal emotional state between turns. The two programs were connected to each other over ARPANET, producing one of the earliest conversations between two machines.
